The Math (Simplified)

A qubit state is written as:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

Where |α|² + |β|² = 1 (probabilities sum to 100%)

On the Bloch sphere:

α = cos(θ/2)
β = sin(θ/2) · eiφ

θ = polar angle (0° to 180°)
φ = azimuthal angle (0° to 360°)

🌍 Visualization

Bloch Sphere

The Bloch sphere is a geometric representation of a qubit's state. Every point on the sphere corresponds to a unique quantum state.

Key Points

  • |0⟩ — North pole (top). Pure state, θ = 0°
  • |1⟩ — South pole (bottom). Pure state, θ = 180°
  • |+⟩ — Equator (right). Equal superposition, 50/50
  • Superposition — Any point between poles is a superposition
  • Measurement collapses the state to either |0⟩ or |1⟩

Quantum Gates Explained

Quantum gates manipulate qubit states. Each gate rotates the qubit on the Bloch sphere in a specific way.

H
Hadamard Gate
Creates superposition. Maps |0⟩ to |+⟩ (equal mix of 0 and 1). The most important gate for quantum computing.

Rotation: 180° around X+Z axis
Effect: Creates 50/50 superposition
X
Pauli-X Gate (NOT)
The quantum equivalent of a classical NOT gate. Flips |0⟩ to |1⟩ and vice versa. A 180° rotation around the X axis.

[ 1 0 ]
Rotation: 180° around X axis

Z
Pauli-Z Gate
Applies a phase flip. Leaves |0⟩ unchanged but flips the sign of |1⟩. A 180° rotation around the Z axis.

[ 0 -1 ]
Rotation: 180° around Z axis
Effect: Phase flip (|1⟩ → -|1⟩)

CNOT Gate
The "Controlled NOT" gate. Flips the target qubit only when the control qubit is |1⟩. Essential for creating entanglement.

A 90° rotation around the Z axis. Also called the "phase gate". It applies a quarter-turn phase shift.

🔗 Deep Dive

Quantum Entanglement

Entanglement is a quantum phenomenon where two or more particles become correlated in such a way that the quantum state of each particle cannot be described independently — even when separated by large distances.

“I would not call [entanglement] one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics.”
— Erwin Schrödinger, 1935

EPR Paradox

In 1935, Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen published a thought experiment arguing that quantum mechanics must be incomplete. They proposed that “hidden variables” must exist to explain the correlations between entangled particles, since the alternative — instantaneous influence across any distance — seemed impossible.

Bell’s Theorem

In 1964, John Bell proved that no theory of local hidden variables can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics. Bell inequalities provide an experimental test: if violated, entanglement is real and nature is genuinely non-local. Decades of experiments have confirmed Bell inequality violations, ruling out local hidden variable theories.

Spooky Action at a Distance

Einstein famously derided entanglement as “spooky action at a distance.” When two entangled qubits are measured, the result of one instantly determines the other, regardless of the distance between them. However, this does not allow faster-than-light communication — the no-communication theorem guarantees that entanglement alone cannot transmit information, preserving relativity.

2022 Nobel Prize in Physics

Alain Aspect, John F. Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger were awarded the Nobel Prize for their experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and pioneering quantum information science. Their work proved that entanglement is a real, measurable phenomenon — not just a theoretical curiosity.

📚 Milestones

History of Quantum Computing

From early theoretical proposals to modern quantum processors, the journey of quantum computing spans over four decades of breakthroughs.

1980

Quantum Turing Machine

Paul Benioff introduces the concept of a quantum Turing machine, providing the first rigorous description of a quantum mechanical model of computation.

1982

Feynman’s Proposal

Richard Feynman proposes that a quantum computer could efficiently simulate quantum physics — something classical computers struggle with exponentially.

1984

Quantum Key Distribution

Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard propose the BB84 protocol, demonstrating that quantum mechanics can be used for secure communication via quantum key distribution.

1985

Universal Quantum Computer

David Deutsch describes a universal quantum computer that can simulate any physical process, establishing the theoretical foundation for quantum computing.

1993

Quantum Teleportation

Charles Bennett and collaborators propose the quantum teleportation protocol, showing how an unknown quantum state can be transmitted using entanglement and classical communication.

1994

Shor’s Algorithm

Peter Shor develops a polynomial-time algorithm for factoring large integers on a quantum computer, threatening modern RSA encryption and proving quantum computers’ practical advantage.

1996

Grover’s Search Algorithm

Lov Grover develops a quantum search algorithm that provides a quadratic speedup for unstructured search problems — finding an item in an unsorted database in √N steps instead of N.

1998

First 2-Qubit Quantum Computer

Researchers demonstrate the first experimental 2-qubit quantum computer using nuclear magnetic resonance, performing simple quantum algorithms in a physical system.

2019

Quantum Supremacy

Google claims quantum supremacy with their 54-qubit Sycamore processor, performing a specific computation in 200 seconds that would take the world’s fastest supercomputer approximately 10,000 years.

2022

Nobel Prize in Physics

Alain Aspect, John F. Clauser, and Anton Zeilinger receive the Nobel Prize for pioneering experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and advancing quantum information science.

2024

Willow Error Correction

Google’s Willow quantum chip achieves below-threshold quantum error correction, demonstrating that adding more qubits can actually reduce error rates — a critical milestone for building fault-tolerant quantum computers.
